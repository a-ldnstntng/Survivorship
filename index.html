Population Ecology: Survivorship Analysis from Cemetery Data

A Life Table Study on Male and Female Populations

Investigator Ali Andrei B.S. Computer Science
Course / Lab Group Biology 102: Population Ecology Section 4A — Lab Group Delta
Date of Submission May 21, 2026 Semester II, Academic Year 2025-2026
Research Focus

Objectives & Key Goals

This laboratory investigation is designed to analyze human cohorts using demographic principles usually applied to natural wildlife populations.

01

Collect Historical Mortality Data

Gather a statistically significant sample size of 400 individuals (200 males, 200 females) from a local cemetery database, spanning both historical and modern eras.

02

Construct Standard Life Tables

Build cohort life tables for both male and female populations, tracking age-specific intervals of 5 years from birth to extreme senescence.

03

Compute Survivorship & Mortality

Apply demographic mathematical models to calculate survivorship (lx), mortality rate (qx), and age-specific life expectancy (ex) for each cohort.

04

Generate Comparative Curves

Render and evaluate comparative survivorship curves to identify the ecological survivorship models (Type I, II, or III) exhibited by human cohorts.

Scientific Method

Methodology & Analytical Pipeline

To ensure empirical rigor and demographic accuracy, the study was executed using the following structured scientific workflow.

01

Cohort Selection

Exactly 400 individuals were randomly selected from headstones: 200 biological males and 200 biological females. To prevent cohort bias, samples were drawn uniformly across several cemetery sections.

02

Age Calculation

For each individual, gender, birth year, and death year were logged. The precise age at death was calculated as: Age = Death Year - Birth Year. Infant mortalities under 1 year were logged as Age 0.

03

Age Grouping

Individuals were categorized into 5-year class intervals: 0-4, 5-9, 10-14, ... through to 100+. The frequency of deaths within each interval formed the d_x parameter.

04

Demographic Parsing

Using raw d_x counts, an algorithmic parser calculated cohort metrics (n_x, l_x, q_x, L_x, T_x, and e_x) utilizing standard ecological life table equations.

Key Demography Calculations Applied

Survivorship (lx) lx = nx / n0
Mortality Rate (qx) qx = dx / nx
Life Interval Lived (Lx) Lx = 5 × (nx + nx+1) / 2
Life Expectancy (ex) ex = Tx / nx

Demographic Analysis & Discussion

GUIDE QUESTION 1

What can you say about the populations analyzed? What are the observable trends among age groups?

The datasets for both male and female populations show a demographic pattern highly characteristic of modernizing or modern human cohorts. The core observable trends are:

  • Extremely Low Juvenile Mortality: Survivorship (lx) remains above 90% through childhood and early adulthood (intervals 0-4 to 25-29). This indicates effective parental care, baseline sanitation, and protection from early environmental hazards.
  • Prolonged Adult Stability: A long plateau of low mortality is visible between the ages of 15 and 55. The slope of the curve is extremely flat in this range, indicating that the probability of survival during prime reproductive and working years is high.
  • Late-Onset Senescence: A clear, rapid escalation of deaths occurs after age 60, with the slope of both curves steepening dramatically. Over 75% of the total cohort deaths occur between ages 60 and 85, displaying physiological senescence.
  • Female Longevity Bias: Female cohorts consistently display higher survivorship at every age interval compared to males. The female median lifespan is roughly 6.7 years longer, representing a universal physiological and behavioral pattern seen in demography.
GUIDE QUESTION 2

What type of survivorship curve is exhibited? Why does this cohort exhibit this curve?

Both populations exhibit a classic, robust Type I Survivorship Curve (highly convex). A Type I curve is defined by high survivorship throughout early and middle life, followed by a rapid, concentrated decline in survival during old age.

This pattern is exhibited because humans are highly K-selected species characterized by:

  • High Parental Investment: Few offspring are produced, but massive physiological, social, and economic resources are dedicated to their nurture and protection.
  • Advanced Social Infrastructure: Communities provide safe shelter, clean drinking water, regulated sanitation, and reliable food supplies, which buffer individuals from natural selection factors (e.g. predators, famines).
  • Modern Medicine & Public Health: Vaccines, antibiotics, clinical interventions, and emergency services prevent pathogens and physical traumas from causing widespread youth mortality, pushing deaths into the late physiological limit.
GUIDE QUESTION 3

If you were to compare two cemeteries, such as a historic one (pre-1900) and a modern active one, what differences would you expect in their survivorship curves and why?

If comparing a historical cohort (interred pre-1900) to a modern cohort (interred post-1950), we would observe dramatic shifts in the curvature and metrics:

Pre-1900 Historic Cemetery

  • Shape: Approaches a Type II (linear) or Type III (highly concave) in early intervals before leveling out.
  • Infant Mortality: Massive spike (20-30% deaths in the 0-4 age group) due to a lack of antibiotics, sanitation, sterile birthing, and vaccinations.
  • Median Lifespan: Considerably lower (often 35-45 years).
  • Causes: High infectious disease rates (tuberculosis, cholera, smallpox) and high maternal mortality.

Post-1950 Modern Cemetery

  • Shape: A perfect, high-plateau Type I curve with a rectangularized profile.
  • Infant Mortality: Negligible (well under 1%), forming a nearly flat line at 1.0 survivorship up to age 50.
  • Median Lifespan: Elevated to 75-82 years.
  • Causes: Advanced public health systems, massive medical infrastructure, robust pediatric care, and treatments for chronic age-related conditions.

Demographic Investigation Summary

Key Takeaways from our Survivorship Analysis

Ecological Theory Confirmed

Human cohorts demonstrate a strong, unequivocal Type I survivorship pattern, indicating high adaptation to ecological niches and substantial parental buffering of infant life.

Sex-Based Longevity Gap

Female cohorts display higher survivorship and longer life expectancy at almost all age intervals, pointing to combined genetic, biological, and behavioral advantages.

CS & Biology Synergy

Demographic research is highly enhanced by computer science models. Constructing interactive life tables and charts provides dynamic, real-time insights into environmental health variables.
